How do we get OTEP to launch with a catalogue people can actually use?

A working session to align on the problems we're actually solving, ideate on solutions, and make the decisions we've been deferring.

Before we start
We've completed a full ingestion analysis. The headline: 473 of 633 open opportunities (75%) will not ingest today. This isn't just a data quality problem — it surfaces three harder questions about our product, our process, and our relationship with agencies. This session is about those questions.

Context — what we know

The ingestion pipeline reads from the OTG source file and imports opportunities into OTEP. A opportunity is blocked if any of 7 required fields is missing or its type tag is unrecognised. We cannot modify the creation form at source. The remediation path today is: analysis → Excel report → distribute to agencies → agencies fix → re-ingest.

The numbers that should concern us most aren't the 473 blocked opportunities. They're:

  • 160 — the number of opportunities that would appear on OTEP at launch. Is that enough?
  • 346 — open opportunities with a past end date. Ingesting these makes OTEP look abandoned.
  • 0 — number of agency admins who currently know their opportunity was blocked.
  • ∞ — how many new opportunities will arrive with the same problems after launch, with no gate to stop them.

Design principles for this session

Agree on these before ideating. They'll help us reject ideas that feel good but don't hold up.

1
Agencies are partners, not a compliance problem

The remediation report treats data quality as an agency failure. Better framing: we haven't given agencies the tools to succeed. Solutions should reduce friction for them, not add governance overhead.

2
2
A thin catalogue at launch is worse than a delayed launch

160 opportunities across all of government will feel empty, especially after filtering. If users search for Gig opportunities in a specific function and get 3 results, they'll conclude OTEP isn't for them. We should define a minimum viable catalogue before setting a go-live date.

3
Fix the recurring problem, not just the current instance

Even if agencies fix all 473 opportunities today, the next batch of opportunities posted without a [prefix] will hit the same wall. Solutions should have a "prevention" layer, not just a "remediation" layer.

4
The ingestion logic is temporary scaffolding

Deriving type from a free-text name prefix via regex is a workaround, not a data model. Every solution we build on top of it inherits its fragility. We should be planning to replace it, not extending it.

Problem areas — trio perspectives + ideas

Five problem areas surfaced from the analysis. For each: how PM, Designer, and Engineer see it differently — then our "how might we" and candidate solutions.

Problem area 1 of 5
Agencies have no feedback loop — they don't know their opportunities are blocked
0 agencies currently notified
Product Manager

This is a trust problem. If an agency posts a opportunity, waits two weeks, and it never appears — they'll stop using OTG. We need to close the loop within hours, not weeks. Who owns communicating this? Is it the ingestion pipeline, the platform team, or OTG?

Designer

An Excel report is not a feedback loop — it's a post-mortem. The right moment to surface a problem is when the opportunity is created, not weeks later. Even if we can't touch the creation form, could we surface validation in a pre-submission preview or a opportunity management dashboard?

Engineer

The ingestion pipeline already knows why each opportunity was blocked — we're just not doing anything with that output. We could write failures to a database and serve them via a simple API. The engineering effort to notify is lower than the effort to build the analysis we just did.

"How might we surface ingestion failures to agency admins before they even ask?"
Quick win (days) Medium (weeks) Big bet (months)
Automated email per opportunity owner on ingestion failure Quick
Ingestion pipeline emails GigOwner directly with the specific field(s) missing. No new UI needed. Works within existing email infrastructure. Risk: email fatigue if agencies have many blocked opportunities.
Agency admin dashboard — "Your opportunities on OTEP" Medium
A read-only web view per agency showing all their opportunities: which are live, which are blocked (and why), which are stale. No write access needed — just visibility. Would reduce the need for the remediation Excel entirely.
Batch remediation digest — weekly per agency Medium
Instead of an ad-hoc Excel file, generate a styled HTML email digest every Monday showing each agency their current blocked opportunities. Owned by the pipeline, not the platform team. Scales automatically.
Inline validation in OTG at point of opportunity creation Big bet
Requires touching the source creation form — currently out of scope. Worth flagging as the right long-term fix. Would prevent the problem entirely rather than detect it post-hoc. Track as future work with OTG product owner.
Problem area 2 of 5
The type classification system is too fragile to maintain
163 opportunities unclassifiable today · grows with every new tag variant
Product Manager

Every time an agency invents a new tag like [OCMO 2026] or [Innofest], we have to update the regex and re-run classification. That's not a product — that's ongoing maintenance. We need to own the taxonomy, not react to it.

Designer

The [prefix] convention was never designed — it emerged organically. Agencies are using it to communicate programme context ([KidSTART], [agilePSD-LDS]), not opportunity type. We're asking one field to do two different jobs. Those should be separate.

Engineer

The regex approach has a ceiling. We have 21 unrecognised tag variants today. In 12 months we'll have 50. The right model is a controlled lookup table — a list of approved tags mapped to canonical types — maintained by a product owner, not hardcoded in a script.

"How might we own the tag taxonomy rather than react to whatever agencies create?"
Publish a canonical tag list and communicate it to agencies Quick
A one-page reference doc: approved [prefix] tags, what type they map to, what each type means. Circulate to all agency admins. Doesn't fix existing opportunities but prevents new variants from being invented.
Tag lookup table in the ingestion pipeline — admin-editable Medium
Replace the regex with a database table: tag_pattern → canonical_type. A non-engineer can add "[KidSTART] = Secondment" without a code deploy. Unknown tags are surfaced for classification rather than silently TBC'd.
Classify unrecognised tags with a short BO review workflow Medium
New unknown tags are queued for the BO to classify once. Once classified, all opportunities with that tag are retroactively updated. Prevents the 109-opportunity TBC problem from recurring.
Structured Type field in OTG creation form Big bet
The permanent fix. A dropdown with approved types means no free-text prefix parsing needed. Requires OTG product owner buy-in and form change. Could be phased: add the field as optional first, then make mandatory.
Problem area 3 of 5
346 stale opportunities will pollute the catalogue if ingested
55% of open opportunities have a past end date · 255 have zero applicants
Product Manager

Stale listings destroy applicant trust faster than an empty catalogue. One "Application closed 14 months ago" listing and the user questions everything. We need a clear policy before go-live: do we ingest stale opportunities, flag them, or exclude them?

Designer

There are two types of "stale": accidentally stale (agency forgot to close it) and intentionally open-ended (standing roles with no fixed end date). These need different UX treatments. An evergreen posting shouldn't require a fake end date just to pass validation.

Engineer

Staleness is easy to compute — GigEnd < today AND Status = Open. The harder question is what the pipeline does with the result. Exclude entirely: simple. Flag for applicant visibility: requires a OTEP listing-page change. Auto-close: requires a write-back to OTG. Three very different engineering scopes.

"How might we protect the applicant experience from stale listings without penalising agencies that use rolling end dates?"
Hard exclude stale opportunities from ingestion (GigEnd < today) Quick
Simplest pipeline rule. Pros: clean catalogue, no stale listings. Cons: excludes legitimate evergreen roles. Mitigate by adding a "Evergreen / No fixed end date" checkbox to the creation form — but that requires a form change.
Auto-exclude stale + zero-applicant opportunities only Quick
More permissive: only exclude opportunities that are both past their end date AND have never received a single application. 255 opportunities would be excluded. Opportunities with at least one applicant stay in, on the assumption someone intentionally kept them open.
Surface "Closing soon" / "Recently closed" state on listing card Medium
Ingest stale opportunities but show a visual indicator ("Applications may have closed") on the OTEP card. Lets applicants self-select. Requires a listing page UI change. Puts the decision with the applicant, not the pipeline.
Automated stale opportunity notification to agency admin Medium
30 days before expiry: "Your opportunity [X] closes in 30 days — extend or close it in OTG." Prevents the backlog from growing. Requires knowing who the admin is (GigOwner) and a reliable email channel.
Problem area 4 of 5
160 opportunities may not be enough to launch with
No minimum viable catalogue threshold defined
Product Manager

We've never defined what "enough" looks like. 160 total opportunities is already thin. After filtering by agency, type, or function, some views will return single digits. We need to decide: is there a minimum count before we go live, and if so, who unblocks it?

Designer

The problem isn't just count — it's distribution. Even 400 opportunities is fine if they're spread across types and functions. But 160 opportunities that are 70% Secondments from two agencies means most filter combinations return nothing. We should model what the filtered experience looks like, not just the total.

Engineer

We can model the catalogue at any threshold. If we know which agencies are willing to remediate, we can estimate the realistic passing count post-cleanup. The engineering question is how frequently we can re-run ingestion — if it's daily, agencies can fix and see results quickly.

"How might we define 'good enough to launch' before we pick a go-live date?"
Define minimum viable catalogue: count × distribution Quick
Decision, not engineering. Propose a threshold — e.g. 300 passing opportunities, at least 3 opportunity types with 20+ opportunities each. Get BO and leadership sign-off before committing to a go-live date.
Target-agency remediation blitz before launch Quick
Enterprise Singapore alone = 178 blocked opportunities. A single focused session with their admin team unlocks 38% of all blocked content. Prioritise the top 3 agencies (EnterpriseGov, MTI, MSF) for white-glove remediation support before go-live.
Model catalogue distribution at each remediation milestone Medium
Run a simulation: what does OTEP look like if only EnterpriseGov fixes their opportunities? What if MTI also fixes? Build a heat-map of opportunity coverage by type × function so designers can validate the browse experience at each milestone.
Problem area 5 of 5
Ingestion is a one-time event in our heads, but a recurring operation in practice
Sync cadence, update handling, and ownership undefined
Product Manager

We've been talking about "the ingestion" as if it happens once. But opportunities are created, edited, closed, and re-opened continuously. Who runs the ingestion after launch — and how often? If an agency fixes a opportunity on Tuesday, when does it appear on OTEP? If the answer is "next month", we have a product gap.

Designer

From an applicant's perspective: if I apply for a opportunity on Monday and it closes on Wednesday, am I notified? If an agency edits the role description after ingestion, does OTEP reflect it? These are continuity-of-experience questions that the current pipeline design doesn't answer.

Engineer

There are three models: (1) full re-ingest on a schedule — simple but slow; (2) delta sync based on DateModified — efficient but requires reliable timestamps; (3) event-driven on OTG save — real-time but requires OTG to emit events. The choice affects how quickly fixes appear on OTEP.

"How might we make OTEP feel live — not like a monthly snapshot?"
Daily scheduled full re-ingest Quick
Run the full pipeline every night. Opportunities fixed by an agency appear on OTEP within 24 hours. Simple to operate; no need to track which opportunities changed. Acceptable latency for most use cases. Start here.
Delta sync on DateModified — near real-time updates Medium
On each run, only process opportunities where DateModified > last_run_timestamp. Reduces processing load. Requires DateModified to be reliably updated in OTG (verify this assumption — some edits may not touch it).
Define and communicate the sync SLA to agencies Quick
Whatever cadence we choose, tell agencies explicitly: "Fixes made before 6pm will appear on OTEP by 9am the next morning." Sets expectations and gives agencies confidence the system is working.

North star for the quarter

What good looks like
By end of Q3: OTEP launches with a catalogue of at least 350 passing opportunities across a minimum of 4 opportunity types. Agency admins receive automated feedback within 24 hours of a opportunity failing ingestion. The ingestion pipeline runs daily. The tag taxonomy is owned by a named person and documented in a canonical reference. Stale opportunities are automatically excluded from the catalogue. The plan to move to a structured Type field in OTG is on the roadmap.
The longer game
The ingestion pipeline is temporary. The permanent solution is a reliable data model in OTG — a Type dropdown, mandatory fields enforced at save, and a real-time event stream to OTEP. Every decision we make in this session should be reversible when that day comes. Build the pipeline in a way that can be deprecated cleanly.
